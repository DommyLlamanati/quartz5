**January 6, 2021, was a violent riot at the U.S. Capitol during the electoral vote certification, preceded by months of Trump and allies challenging the 2020 election results through lawsuits, pressure campaigns, and the alternate-elector strategy. It was not a successful “coup” that seized power—Congress certified Biden’s win the same day, the military stayed out, and the transfer of power occurred on schedule—but it involved real violence, injuries, deaths linked to the events, and criminal convictions including seditious conspiracy for some organized groups.**

Here is a factual bullet breakdown:

- **2020 election outcome and early challenges (November 2020 onward)**: Biden won the popular vote and Electoral College. Trump and allies filed dozens of lawsuits alleging irregularities or fraud; nearly all were dismissed or withdrawn for lack of evidence, standing, or procedural reasons. Multiple state and federal officials (including Republican ones), Trump’s own appointees, and courts rejected claims of outcome-changing fraud. Trump continued publicly claiming the election was stolen.

- **Pressure campaigns and alternate electors**: Trump contacted state officials (notably the Georgia call urging officials to “find” votes). Allies organized alternate/“fake” elector certificates in several battleground states, framed by participants as contingent on successful legal challenges. These were submitted in some cases. Critics and later prosecutors treated them as attempts to obstruct the certification process. Historical use of alternate electors in contested elections existed but was not typically prosecuted as criminal conspiracy when framed as contingent.

- **Pressure on Vice President Pence and Congress**: Trump and lawyers (including John Eastman) advanced theories that Pence could reject or return electors on January 6. Pence publicly and privately refused, citing the Constitution and Electoral Count Act. Trump also urged members of Congress to object to state certifications.

- **The January 6 rally and Capitol breach**: Trump held a rally near the White House, speaking for over an hour, repeating election fraud claims, saying “we’re going to walk down to the Capitol,” and using phrases such as “fight like hell.” A large crowd moved to the Capitol. Barriers were breached; some participants used force against police (battering with poles, chemical spray, etc.). Roughly 140 officers were injured. The building was entered; lawmakers evacuated. Congress resumed and completed certification that evening.

- **Deaths and violence scale**: Ashli Babbitt was shot and killed by Capitol Police while climbing through a broken window toward the Speaker’s Lobby. Other deaths associated with the day included medical emergencies in the crowd and later Officer Brian Sicknick (ruled natural causes). Pipe bombs were placed near party headquarters the night before (still unsolved as of earlier reports). Organized elements from groups such as the Proud Boys and Oath Keepers were later convicted of seditious conspiracy for planning to use force to oppose the transfer of power. Thousands of ordinary attendees were present; federal charges focused on those who entered restricted areas, assaulted officers, or planned violence (over 1,500 charged in total across related cases).

- **Trump’s response during the events**: Trump watched coverage, delayed calling for the crowd to leave for hours despite requests from aides and officials, and issued messages that mixed criticism of the violence with continued election claims. He later told the crowd to go home “in peace.”

- **Aftermath prosecutions**: The Department of Justice charged hundreds for specific crimes (trespass, assault, obstruction). Some received lengthy sentences for violence or conspiracy. The House Select Committee (majority Democrat) produced a report blaming Trump as the central cause. Special Counsel Jack Smith later indicted Trump on election-related charges; that case was dismissed after the 2024 election.

### Arctic Frost and Rampart Twelve

These were FBI/DOJ investigative codenames tied to post-January 6 probes into election challenges and related activity. Later Republican-led congressional oversight (Grassley et al.) released documents portraying them as expansive, politically motivated, and weak on predication; defenders described them as legitimate responses to obstruction efforts.

- **[[Rampart Twelve]] (opened ~January 22, 2021)**: Preliminary investigation focused on certain Republican House members (including Boebert, Gosar, Biggs, Brooks) over claims they conducted “reconnaissance” Capitol tours before January 6 that aided the riot. Origins traced to public statements and a letter from Democratic members alleging suspicious visitors. Internal records later showed agents reviewed video and other material, found no evidence linking the members to assisting entrants, and noted doubts about the strength of the allegations. Phone toll records were obtained for some. Oversight materials describe it as a predecessor effort that lacked substantiation yet remained open for a period.

- **Arctic Frost (opened April 2022)**: Broader FBI investigation into alleged conspiracy to obstruct Congress’s certification via the alternate-elector certificates and related pressure. Predicated on the view that submitting the certificates and efforts involving Pence constituted corrupt obstruction. It was transferred to Special Counsel Jack Smith and formed the basis for his election-interference indictment of Trump. Documents show extensive subpoenas, interviews, and toll-record analysis covering periods around January 6 for multiple Republican figures, including senators and House members (toll data shows call times, numbers, duration, and approximate location—not content). Oversight releases indicate hundreds of Republican individuals and organizations were examined. Critics (including later FBI leadership and Senate investigators) called the opening justification thin, noted involvement of agents later criticized for bias, and highlighted overreach on congressional records under Speech or Debate concerns. The underlying Trump case did not result in a trial.

**Bottom line on severity**: The riot interrupted a constitutional process, injured officers, produced deaths tied to the day, and resulted in serious federal convictions for violence and (for some organizers) seditious conspiracy. The preceding months involved sustained pressure and novel legal strategies to reverse certified results that courts had rejected. Labeling the entire sequence a “months-long attempted coup” is a political characterization used by critics; the concrete outcomes were a failed pressure campaign, a violent disruption that was cleared the same day, and subsequent criminal cases against participants and (temporarily) against Trump. Claims of a coordinated, top-down military or government seizure of power lack supporting evidence in the public record. Election challenges themselves are not inherently criminal; violence and proven false certificates submitted as authentic are.
